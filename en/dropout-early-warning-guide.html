Dropout early warning: what it is and how to implement it

Last updated: November 2026

Spotting a student at risk in time is only half the job. This guide explains the parts of an early warning system, what rigorous studies say and how to set one up so alerts do not go unanswered.

In short

  • An early warning system spots students who show signs of risk in time and makes sure every alert reaches a person who acts on it.
  • It consists of five parts: indicators, thresholds, owners, intervention and evaluation. Without the last three, the system is reduced to a report.
  • The available evidence is mixed: in a trial with 73 schools, chronic absence and course failure went down, but credits earned did not; in another with more than 10,000 university students, graduation did not change.
  • The factor that weighs most is the institution's capacity to respond: who reaches out, how fast and what is offered.

What an early warning system is

Defining the concept, a dropout early warning system frequently measures the indicators that anticipate dropout, flags the students who cross an established threshold and assigns each case to a person responsible for handling it. The key word is early, since the alert has to arrive while there is still room to change the outcome, not once the report card has confirmed it.

It should be distinguished from a predictive model: a model only estimates the probability that a student will leave, while an early warning system covers the whole process that turns that risk into a conversation with the student and records what happened next.

Looking at Mexico, the Ministry of Education's National Higher Education Program 2026-2030 notes that 3 out of 10 in-person undergraduate students drop out during their first year and lists, among its responses, strengthening early warning systems and personalized academic support.

The five parts

The system is made up of five parts that work as a continuous cycle:

  • Indicators. What is measured for each student: logins to the platform, submissions, interim grades, attendance and outstanding balances. They should update weekly or daily, not only at the end of the term.
  • Threshold. The point at which a student is flagged. Comparing the student with their own group is more accurate than a fixed number, since every course keeps its own pace.
  • Owner. Every alert requires someone to pick it up and a deadline to do so: the instructor, a tutor, wellbeing or finance, depending on the reason.
  • Intervention. What the student is offered: a conversation, tutoring, a payment arrangement or a referral. An alert without an intervention does not change the outcome.
  • Evaluation. Recording every contact and its outcome, and reviewing at the end of the term which alerts were right and which actions helped.

What the evidence says

There are few rigorous studies on the subject, and their results call for caution. Three cases from the United States help gauge what to expect from a system of this kind; the last part summarizes what has been published in Mexico and Latin America.

EWIMS (United States, high schools). The Regional Educational Laboratory Midwest randomly assigned 73 schools in three states: half implemented the EWIMS early warning system during the 2014-15 school year and half waited one year. After that first year, schools using the system reduced chronic absence from 14% to 10% and failure of at least one course from 26% to 21% among ninth and tenth graders. Average GPA rose slightly (from 2.87 to 2.98), although the share of students with a low GPA did not change significantly; no effect was detected on suspensions or credits earned, and the authors themselves note that implementation was limited.

MAAPS (United States, universities). Eleven large public universities tested proactive advising supported by analytics and alerts, randomly assigning more than 10,000 low-income or first-generation students. Evaluating the program six years later, Ithaka S+R found no significant effect on graduation or persistence for the full sample. The exception was Georgia State, the only one that kept the program for all six years and that already had centralized advising and strong alert tools: there, students who received the advising graduated at a rate 7 points higher than the comparison group at the same university. Elsewhere the program lasted three years, and several sites dealt with alert systems that did not push information to advisors, staff turnover and students who did not respond.

Georgia State University. Being the most cited case, the university runs a system that checks more than 700 risk signals daily and alerts its advisors, and reported that the whole university's graduation rate rose 6 points since 2013. This figure differs from MAAPS: it compares the university with itself over time, not against a control group. At the same time, the university hired 42 additional advisors, and more than 90% of the project's cost was staff, not technology.

Mexico and Latin America. In the region, the most common institutional response has been tutoring. Since 2000, ANUIES, the national association of Mexican universities, has proposed that every institution run a tutoring program to raise persistence and completion, and SEP's national higher education program again calls for academic support. However, published evaluations usually measure participant satisfaction or a model's accuracy, not its effect on dropout. The evaluation of the undergraduate tutoring program at UNAM's School of Veterinary Medicine, for example, measured tutor satisfaction; in Colombia, the Universidad Tecnológica de Pereira built an early warning model on first-semester data that identified 61.97% of the students who later dropped out, without the study measuring its effect on persistence. The search carried out for this guide found no study in the region with a comparison group like EWIMS or MAAPS.

Concluding from these cases, an alert on its own does not retain any student; the people who respond to it do. A system is only as good as the institution's capacity to handle its alerts in time.

How to implement one in six steps

The following implementation steps are proposed:

  1. Define what you want to prevent, and for whom. For example, dropout during the first year of an undergraduate program. Setting a concrete goal makes it possible to choose indicators and later measure whether the system worked.
  2. Choose indicators you already have. The learning platform (LMS) records logins, submissions and interim grades; the registrar, enrollment; finance, balances. Starting with what updates automatically, without manual entry, is recommended. How to spot at-risk students in Moodle shows where each signal lives.
  3. Set thresholds and review them weekly. Compare each student with their group; it is better to start with a few well-grounded alerts than with many that staff cannot keep up with.
  4. Give every alert an owner and a deadline. For example, 48 hours for the first contact. If nobody picks it up, the alert escalates to coordination or leadership.
  5. Record every contact and its outcome. Channel, date, what was offered and whether the student started submitting again, since without that record there is no way to know what works.
  6. Evaluate at the end of the term. Review which alerts were right and which actions helped and, where possible, turn alerts on in some groups before others to have a point of comparison. The indicators for doing so are explained in Student retention indicators.

Common mistakes

The mistakes that most often undermine these systems are the following:

  • Alerts without an owner. A report everyone sees and nobody handles.
  • Waiting for the grade. The report card confirms the problem weeks after the student's activity had already changed.
  • Too many alerts. When staff cannot keep up with them, they stop opening them.
  • Labeling instead of supporting. The signal is a reason to talk, not a sanction or a judgment about the student. Jisc's Code of practice for learning analytics recommends transparency with students and that interventions always be supportive.
  • Not measuring the effect. Without a record of outcomes, the system cannot learn or be justified to leadership.
