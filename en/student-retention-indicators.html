Student retention indicators: which to track and how to calculate them

Last updated: November 2026

To improve retention you first need to measure it the same way every term. This guide gathers the ANUIES and SEP definitions, a worked example and the process indicators that let you act in time.

In short

  • Retention is measured by cohort: of the students who started together, how many are still enrolled one term, one year or a whole program later.
  • The classic indicators are first-to-second-year retention, dropout per school year and completion rate.
  • All of them arrive late, since they tell how many left; to act in time, process indicators such as time to first contact with a student at risk are required.
  • In Mexico, 3 out of 10 students who start an in-person undergraduate program drop out during the first year, so that is the indicator worth watching most closely.

Retention, persistence, dropout

Although Mexican universities use these terms almost interchangeably, it helps to tell them apart when measuring:

  • Retention or persistence (permanencia): the student is still enrolled at the institution or in the program, which is what an institution seeks to increase.
  • Dropout during a term (abandono): the student stops enrolling during a school year. Mexico's Ministry of Education (SEP) publishes it as a percentage per school year.
  • Attrition (deserción): dropout that becomes permanent. In practice it is calculated by subtracting retention from 100%: if retention is 74%, attrition is 26%.

1. First-to-second-year retention

It is the most useful indicator for most institutions, given that the first year concentrates dropout. ANUIES (the Mexican association of universities) defines it as the percentage of students in a cohort who remain in the program or institution in their second year, relative to those who enrolled the previous year. In a semester-based program the second year starts in the third semester, which is why the formula counts students enrolled in that semester.

Retention = (cohort students enrolled in the third semester ÷ students who entered in the cohort) × 100

Illustrative example: 400 students enroll and a year later 296 are still enrolled. Retention is 296 ÷ 400 × 100 = 74%, while first-year attrition is the remaining 26%.

The Universidad de Sonora's technical sheet notes two precautions: use adjusted first-year enrollment, excluding provisional students and redistributing program changes, and to calculate the indicator for both the institution and the starting program, since a student who switches programs is still at the institution but left the program.

At institutions with four-month terms, the same can be measured per term: of the students who started in September, how many re-enroll in January and how many in May.

2. Dropout per school year

Defined as the percentage of students who leave school during a school year per 100 enrolled, it is the indicator SEP publishes in its national statistics: in higher education, the dropout rate was 7.1% in the 2023-2024 school year, down from 7.2% the year before. While it allows benchmarking against the system, it mixes cohorts and does not show at what point in the program students left.

3. Completion rate

This indicator, expressed as the number of students who graduate from a level in a given year per 100 who enrolled in the starting cohort (eficiencia terminal), summarizes the whole trajectory and is usually the one accreditors ask for. However, it takes years to reflect any change, so what is done this semester will show up in the completion rate four or five years from now.

How to read a cohort

A cohort table puts each entering class in a row and the following terms in columns, with the percentage still enrolled. Read by columns, it shows whether recent classes are retained better than earlier ones; read by rows, it reveals in which term most students leave.

Process indicators: the ones you can act on

Considering that the three indicators above only report how many students left, measuring the process is also proposed to know whether the institution is reacting in time:

  • Time to first contact: from when a student at risk is detected until someone writes to or calls them. How to organize that follow-up is covered in the early warning guide.
  • Share of alerts handled within the deadline the institution set.
  • Re-engagement: how many of the contacted students open the material or submit again in the following two weeks.
  • Failure rate by course: which courses concentrate students at risk, in order to support the course and not just the student.

What data is needed

This is the information each indicator requires and the area where it usually lives:

Retention and dropoutEnrollment per student and term, program changes and withdrawalsRegistrar

For the first three indicators, a table of enrolled and re-enrolled students per term and level is enough, which is precisely the information used to prepare a retention diagnosis before a pilot.
